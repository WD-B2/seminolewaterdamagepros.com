What water extraction means and why it comes first

Water extraction is the physical removal of liquid water from a building. Crews use truck-mounted or portable extractors, submersible pumps and weighted wands that pull water out of carpet, pad and floor cavities. It is always the first step of the water damage restoration process because every gallon removed as liquid is a gallon the dehumidifiers never have to handle as vapor.

Extraction is not drying. A floor can look nearly clear after extraction and still hold moisture in the subfloor, wall base and pad. That is why extraction is followed by structural drying and dehumidification, where moisture meters tell the crew what is still wet.

Emergency water removal: what to do in the first hour

  1. Find the source and stop it. Close the fixture shutoff, or the home's main valve, which our guide on shutting off the water main explains.
  2. Protect yourself. Do not wade through standing water if outlets, cords or appliances are touching it. If the panel is dry and easy to reach, switch off power to the wet area.
  3. Lift what you safely can. Get shoes, papers, small furniture and electronics off wet floors. Put foil or plastic under furniture legs on wet carpet.
  4. Photograph the room, the source and every damaged item before moving it. Your insurer will ask for these.
  5. Call (727) 626-0985. Tell the dispatcher where the water started, how deep it is and which rooms are wet.

The 24/7 line is answered at night, on weekends and on holidays. Same-day response is available when a crew is free, and the dispatcher will say plainly what the schedule looks like instead of quoting an arrival window we cannot keep.

How crews pull water out of different surfaces

The method depends on what is wet. Carpet and pad hold far more water than they appear to, so a weighted extractor is run in slow overlapping passes to press water out of the pad below. Tile and sealed concrete are simpler, because a wet vacuum and squeegee clear most of the surface.

Hardwood is delicate, so crews use low-pressure tools and move on quickly to drying, which is covered on our hardwood floor page.

Deep standing water, like a flooded garage or a crawl space after a heavy summer storm, calls for a pump first and extractors second. Where water has run down a wall cavity, small holes near the baseboard let trapped water drain out so it does not sit on the bottom plate.

The water category changes the job

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard sorts water into three categories. Category 1 comes from a clean source such as a supply line. Category 2 carries some contamination, such as washing machine discharge. Category 3 is grossly contaminated, such as sewage or floodwater. Clean water that sits long enough can degrade into a worse category, which is another reason speed matters.

A Category 3 job is not a pump-and-go job. Porous materials usually come out, and surfaces are cleaned and disinfected. Our sewage backup and flood damage pages cover that process in detail.

Why Seminole's climate raises the stakes

Pinellas County gets roughly 47 inches of rain in an average year and spends much of it humid. Wet drywall and carpet pad that stay damp in that air can support mold growth within a couple of days, which is the 24 to 48 hour window the EPA describes.

Seminole homes built in the decades after the 1960s boom along Park Boulevard often have carpet over concrete slab, so water spreads sideways under the pad before anyone sees it.

Our office on Park Boulevard North sits near Seminole City Center and the Lake Seminole side of town, so a call from Oakhurst, Seminole Lake Country Club or Seminole Forest is a short run on roads we already know. We also dispatch to Indian Rocks Beach and Saint Petersburg.

What affects the price of water extraction

  • The square footage that is wet and the depth of the standing water.
  • Whether the water is Category 1, 2 or 3.
  • How much carpet pad, drywall or insulation must be removed.
  • How many hours of extraction and how many days of drying follow.
  • Whether furniture and contents must be moved or packed out.

Insurance often responds to sudden, accidental water events and often does not respond to long-term seepage. Our guide on whether homeowners insurance covers water damage walks through the usual wording. Read your own policy and ask your insurer, because it is the policy that decides.
