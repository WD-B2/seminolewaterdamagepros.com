How to Check a Home for Water Damage Before Buying in Seminole, FL

Water damage is cheaper to find before closing than after. This walk-through shows what to look at, what to ask and when to bring in a specialist.

Written by the Seminole Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 2026.

Call (727) 626-0985

Why water damage is the hidden cost of older Florida homes

In a humid coastal county, water is the main enemy of a house. Roofs leak, AC drain lines clog, supply lines age and slabs shift. Sellers repaint stains, and a house can look clean while hiding a problem. A careful walk-through, a good inspection and a few pointed questions can save you from buying a repair bill.

We restore water damage and are not home inspectors, so treat this as a checklist of things to notice, then ask a licensed inspector to evaluate what you find.

Start outside

  • Look at the roofline for missing or lifted shingles, stained fascia and failing flashing.
  • Check that gutters and downspouts carry water away from the foundation.
  • Note whether the grade slopes toward or away from the house.
  • Look for cracks in the slab or stucco and staining near the base of walls.
  • Check around hose bibs, the water heater closet and the AC condenser for staining.

EPA guidelines say that controlling moisture is the key to controlling mold, so a dry reading on a suspect wall is more reassuring than a clean surface. Ask the inspector to record readings in the report.

Smell is a useful tool. A musty or earthy odor that returns after cleaning can point to moisture in materials that look dry. Our page on slab leaks lists warm floor spots and meter movement that sometimes show up in inspections.

Check the systems that make water

  1. Water heater

    Look at the age label, the drain pan and the floor around it for rust and stains. Tanks near the end of life can rupture.

  2. AC air handler

    Check the condensate line, the pan and the ceiling below. See our page on AC leak damage.

  3. Attic

    Look at insulation and decking for dark stains, especially around vents and penetrations.

  4. Plumbing

    Run the water, flush toilets and check pressure. Ask about the pipe material, since older galvanized and some plastic lines have known failure patterns.

  5. Meter test

    With all water off, watch the meter. If it moves, there may be a leak.

Ask the seller and the inspector

  • Has the home had any water or flood claim, and can I see the records.
  • Has the roof or plumbing been repaired or replaced, and when.
  • Is flood insurance required, and what is the flood zone for this address.
  • Are there any wind-mitigation or four-point inspection reports.
  • Can I have a moisture meter or thermal camera used during the inspection.
  • Would a mold assessment make sense given what we see.

The FEMA Flood Map Service Center shows the flood zone for any address. Your insurer can tell you how that zone affects cost and requirements.

When to bring in a restoration or mold professional

If the inspector finds elevated moisture, stained framing or visible growth, ask for a written scope before you negotiate. Our structural drying and mold remediation pages describe what that scope looks like. Moisture readings and photos give you leverage in negotiating repairs or credits.

A lender or buyer may later request mold clearance, which is a separate step and not a guarantee of a clean bill of health. We cover the basics of this in our guide to clearance testing.

Red flags that deserve a closer look

  • Fresh paint on one ceiling patch or the lower part of one wall.
  • New flooring in only one room, especially near a bathroom or laundry area.
  • Air fresheners or strong cleaning smells on a showing day.
  • A dehumidifier running in a closet or a garage.
  • Rust stains at the base of the water heater.
  • Window sills with dark staining or soft wood.
  • A seller who is vague about roof age or past repairs.

None of these proves a problem. Each one is a reason to ask a specific question and to get an inspector to test the area with a meter.

Using findings in the negotiation

Written readings, photos and a scope from a restoration company give you something concrete to discuss with the seller. Depending on the contract, you may ask for repairs, a credit or a price change, or you may decide to walk away during the inspection period. Your agent and attorney can explain what your contract allows.

Seminole and Pinellas notes

Seminole has plenty of 1970s to 1990s slab houses with carpet over concrete, popular around Park Boulevard and Seminole Boulevard, and newer subdivisions like Seminole Ridge. Older homes may have original plumbing, original roofs and older HVAC. Ask about roof age and plumbing, because Florida insurers pay close attention to both.

If you are also looking at the barrier island near Indian Rocks Beach or bungalows in Saint Petersburg, raised floors and older materials add questions about crawl space moisture and storm history.
