Mold Clearance Testing for a Home Sale or Loan in Seminole, FL

A buyer, lender or insurer may ask for proof that remediation worked. Here is what clearance testing means and who should do it.

Written by the Seminole Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 2026.

Call (727) 626-0985

What clearance testing is

Clearance testing, also called post-remediation verification, checks whether a space has returned to a normal condition after mold remediation. It usually combines a visual inspection with air or surface sampling, and a laboratory report compares indoor results with outdoor samples or a baseline. The result is a document you can give to a buyer, lender, insurer or property manager.

It is not a promise that mold will never return. It is a snapshot of conditions on the day of sampling. We do not make health claims, and health questions belong with a physician.

When it comes up

  • A buyer's inspection found growth and the seller agreed to remediate.
  • A lender or appraiser requires documentation before closing.
  • An insurer asks for proof as part of a claim.
  • A landlord or property manager needs a record after a tenant complaint.
  • You want independent confirmation after a large remediation.

Who should test

The best practice is for an independent tester, not the remediation contractor, to do the clearance. Separation prevents a conflict of interest, since the contractor is judging its own work. Ask whether the tester holds recognized credentials and whether they follow a published protocol. The ANSI/IICRC S520 standard describes the verification stage of professional remediation.

The sequence

  1. Assess

    An assessor identifies the extent of growth and the moisture source.

  2. Write the scope

    A protocol sets containment, removal and verification.

  3. Remediate

    The contractor follows the scope. See our mold remediation page.

  4. Dry and verify moisture

    Structural drying confirms the area is dry.

  5. Clear

    The assessor inspects, samples and issues a report.

If clearance fails, the contractor corrects the issue and the assessor retests.

Reading the report

  • The scope and sampling locations.
  • How indoor results compare with outdoor and baseline samples.
  • Visual findings and moisture readings.
  • The laboratory's name and methods.
  • The date of testing.

Mold levels outdoors vary by season, which is why comparison matters. A report that gives only a number with no context is hard to interpret.

Cost and timing

Clearance costs depend on the size of the area, the number of samples and the lab turnaround. Build the time into a closing schedule, since lab results can take days.

Why baseline and outdoor samples matter

Mold spores are in outdoor air all the time, and the amount changes with season, weather and location. A clearance report that compares indoor samples with an outdoor sample taken the same day puts the numbers in context. Without that comparison, a result can be misread in either direction. Good assessors explain their sampling plan before they take a sample and describe limitations in the report.

What can make a clearance fail

  • Visible growth or staining that remains after remediation.
  • Moisture readings that are still elevated.
  • Settled dust that was not cleaned thoroughly.
  • A continuing moisture source such as a small leak or poor drainage.
  • Containment that was removed before cleaning was complete.

A failed clearance is not a catastrophe. The contractor corrects the items listed by the assessor, and the area is re-inspected and retested. It is cheaper to find an issue at clearance than after the walls are closed and painted.

Timing with a sale or loan

Ask your agent, lender and insurer what they require before you hire anyone. Some want a particular form of report, some want the assessor to be independent and some accept a contractor's completion certificate. Getting this wrong can cost days at closing. Build in time for drying, remediation, sampling, laboratory turnaround and any re-test.

Keep every document from the process: the assessor's protocol, the contractor's scope and completion notes, drying logs, laboratory reports and the final clearance letter. Hand the full set to your agent or lender, and keep a copy for your own records. If you sell later, a buyer may ask for the same file, and having it ready saves days.

Questions to ask before you schedule anything

  • Does the buyer, lender or insurer require independent testing.
  • Which report format and credentials will they accept.
  • Who pays for the assessor, the contractor and the laboratory.
  • How many days can the closing or claim wait.
  • What happens if the first clearance does not pass.

Answers to these questions shape who you hire and in what order, and they are cheaper to settle at the start than in the middle of a closing.
